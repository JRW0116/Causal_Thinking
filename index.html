Module 2 · The framework

Pearl's Ladder of Causation

Three kinds of question, each needing more than the one below it to answer. Our survey statistics mostly live on Rung 1. Our clients decide on Rungs 2 and 3.

Rung 3 · ImaginingCounterfactuals
“What if I had done X?” Was it X that caused Y?
At LNS: “Would this deal have hit its targets if the thesis had been tested in diligence?” Mike: “Do you really know what happens when you take it away?”
Rung 2 · DoingIntervention
“What if I do X?” What happens to Y if we change X?
At LNS: “If a client deploys OpEx enterprise-wide, will productivity compound?” Answering takes an experiment, or a causal model applied to the data.
Rung 1 · SeeingAssociation
“What if I see X?” How are X and Y related?
At LNS: “Market shapers are 8.8× more likely to run OpEx enterprise-wide.” Survey cross-tabs and benchmarks live here.

Adapted from Judea Pearl & Dana Mackenzie, The Book of Why (2018)

Data alone can't climb the ladder

Pearl's central point: without an experiment, no amount of observational data answers a Rung 2 question on its own. Getting from “seeing” to “doing” takes causal assumptions — a model of what causes what.

  • A survey cross-tab, however large the sample, is Rung 1.
  • Our causal models — the risk-centric network and its success-side mirror — are those assumptions written down, so the data can test them.
  • The model is what lets CDI carry an insight into one client's context: Matt's “adjacency problem.”

How a council research topic climbs (Mehul, CEO Council)

  1. Build the perspectiveKnowledge document, iterated with the council sponsor
  2. Hypothesize the causal modelWrite down what we believe causes what
  3. Field the surveyCollect Rung 1 data built to test the model
  4. Update the modelKeep the links that survive; drop the ones that don't
A cross-tab earns “is associated with.” Only a model the data has tested earns “if you do this.”

Module 3 · Building blocks

Three junctions every causal diagram is built from

Each behaves differently when you “control for” the middle variable. Most errors in Moves 1, 6 and 8 trace back to one of these.

ChainThe mechanism

M carries X's effect to Y. This is the “because”: decision speed → compounding advantage → productivity. Don't control away the mechanism and then conclude X does nothing.

ForkThe confounder

A common cause Z moves X and Y together with no arrow between them. Rapid growth could lift both high tech's investment and its productivity numbers. Account for Z to close the “back door.”

ColliderThe selection trap

X and Y both cause Z. Filter on Z and you manufacture a link. Did our M&A sample include abandoned deals? The “oldest plant” story exists because that plant survived.

Module 5 · The core skill

Eight causal moves

Each one illustrated with something we — or the people in the room — actually said.

1Name the mechanism, not just the multiplier 2Separate symptoms (and surface effects) from causes 3Try to prove yourself wrong 4“Matters” is not “differentiates” 5Find the conditions 6Check the arrow and the confounders 7Think in systems 8State the counterfactual
1
Rung 2

Name the mechanism, not just the multiplier

A multiplier says two things travel together. A mechanism says why — and gives the client something to act on. In Pearl's terms, the mechanism is the mediator in a chain.

Story: decision velocity → compounding advantage

“If I can make the right decision faster than my competition, that gives me a little chunk of advantage over them… even if it's not significant, it's compounding over time.”— Jim Wells, OpEx roundtable

Story: the data-to-action cycle

Data→Information→Insight→Trust→Action

Data and information are largely solved — we have “dashboard fatigue.” Insight → trust → action is the “inference payload” experienced workers carried invisibly, and the part that retires with them. KM roundtable

“An insight that is not acted on is worth nothing.” — Jim Wells

Habit: after every “X× more likely,” finish the sentence with “because…” If you can't, the stat isn't ready for a client.

A surface effect is the visible evidence of a causal relationship: a real, repeatable pattern downstream of the true cause. It shows up reliably in Rung 1 data. Push on it directly and little moves — because the cause is somewhere else.

What pushing the surface does

Initiatives without the discipline to scale stall as pilots: “let's have this pilot, try this thing… no real line organizing it.”

The causal story

Operational discipline — stabilize, standardize, systematize, sustain — lets each deployment scale. Breadth is the evidence it leaves.

Better: “Leaders don't win because they do more. They do more because their operational discipline lets each deployment scale. Build the discipline; breadth follows.”
Surface-effect test: if the client forced X up tomorrow and changed nothing else, would Y move? If it depends on something else being in place, X is the evidence — and that something is the cause.

Try to prove yourself wrong

“Any good causal scientist can prove something right. It's when you disprove something that you know you're really doing it right.”— Mike Carroll
We believedThe data said
More collaboration, and pushing decisions to the lowest level, are always better.“Dead wrong” — unless collaboration is engineered through identified expertise, decision rights and guardrails. Then it matters, and mature decision architecture goes with 3× the odds of being a World's Most Productive company.
Converting intent into execution separates M&A leaders from followers.True everywhere we looked except M&A. Everyone runs a disciplined playbook; leader–follower gaps were 1–3 points. The differentiator sits upstream, in diligence.
Why it sells: saying “we were wrong” in front of COOs made the rest of the findings more credible. The real learning happens when you are wrong and ask why.

Rung 2

Think in systems: trade-offs, buffers, thresholds

Maximize utilization→Buffer gets used up→Shock or demand shift→Fragile, lost agility
“If you have uncertainty, you need to have a buffer… if you use it up managerially, you lock yourself in and you lose agility.”— Council member, operations academic

Diminishing returns

Past parity, the plant-efficiency mindset stops paying; get the network agile and invest in speed and customer intimacy.

Second-order effects

Outsourcing → fragmentation → you can't close the learning loop fast enough → decision latency. (Mike)

Thresholds

KM payoff concentrates at the top maturity tier. Build the case in two stages; don't expect gains in the pilot.

When a client asks “how much more X should we do,” the causal answer often includes “until here, then stop.”
8
Rung 3

State the counterfactual: “compared to what?”

Every finding and every ROI needs the do-nothing path — and a clear view of which failure you're guarding against.

A · Bend the curve

Amgen measures cost of quality per unit against a “do nothing” curve as volume scales — not against last year's spend.

CQO roundtable

B · Avoided growth, not cuts

“I may not have to put in as many future requisitions.” Elsewhere, inspection findings added ~150 FTE of deviation work; the AI case targets that increment.

CQO roundtable participants

C · Decompose the failure

Deals fail because “you should never have done it,” because they were executed badly, or “because it's actually just hard.” Three causes, three fixes.

Council member, COO Council
Ask clients: what happens on the do-nothing path, and which kind of failure is this investment guarding against?

Module 6 · Coaching moments

Where our language drifted from association to causation

Juxtaposition reads as causation, intended or not

What we said · Matt, COO session

“91% of companies… say they're either adopted or going to adopt this… So everybody's doing it, but 85% of companies are less productive.”

What the room heard

“90-something percent of us have adopted AI, and 85% of us are less efficient than we were before we started.”

What the data says · IPI

The 85% is a cohort: competitive operators outside the top ~15%, who averaged about −2% productivity. Nothing in it says AI made anyone less productive.

Better: “Adoption is nearly universal, so adoption alone can't explain who's winning. The question is what the top 15% do differently with it.”

Watch the drift from “more likely” to “the lever”

Our multipliers are Rung 1. “The lever” is a Rung 2 claim — it needs a mechanism and a tested model, and the client needs a way to test it.

As said

“Organizations that… [have] deployed across the enterprise are 8.8 times more likely to be a market shaping company… so this is the big lever.”

Stronger

Market shapers are 8.8× more likely to run OpEx enterprise-wide. Our read on why: a program that lives in a few experts' heads resets when they leave; one built into the way work is done keeps compounding. Test: would your program survive its champion leaving?

As said

“[Those that did both] are 1.4 times more likely to meet their targets.”

Stronger

Assessing what the target does well forces process understanding before close; adopting its practices keeps the thesis honest. The stronger signal is the downside: 2.4× less likely to recalibrate mid-deal, 2.6× less likely to see the legacy business degrade.

As said

“Leaders are two and a half times more likely to run a single consolidated enterprise data platform.”

Stronger

Trust is the gate between insight and action, and one source of truth is what earns it. Leaders are 1.8× more likely to trust and act on their analytics. Layer AI over 18 sources of truth and it tells one plant's truth to another.

The pattern: association → the mechanism we believe → a test the client can run in their own organization.

Module 8 · The toolkit

Seven questions before a number goes on a slide

  1. MechanismCan I finish “because…” in one sentence?
  2. SurfaceIf the client forced this up tomorrow, would the outcome move?
  3. AlternativesWhat else produces this pattern — growth, size, industry mix, regulation, capex?
  4. DirectionCould the arrow run backward?
  5. ConditionsWhen does it stop being true?
  6. VarianceDoes it differentiate, or is it table stakes?
  7. CounterfactualCompared to what? What happens if it's removed?

Keep the verb honest

“co-occurs with,” “is more common among”Rung 1 · any survey cross-tab
“predicts”Rung 1 · holds after accounting for obvious confounders
“drives,” “if you do X, expect Y”Rung 2 · tested causal model, mechanism, survived disconfirmation
“would have,” “was responsible for”Rung 3 · counterfactual reasoning about a specific case

↓ Evidentiary burden rises down the list. When in doubt, use the weaker verb and add the “because.”

Module 10 · Delivery

Explain it in 30 seconds; take a challenge well

Veena, prepping for the council: “If you were to describe in layman's terms… how would you describe that so that people understand how you're getting to where you're getting?”

“The first thing you do as a human is you understand what makes things true. The second thing you do is you say, all right, what breaks them? That's what this is.”— Mike Carroll, COO Council

Then the structure: the outcome sits in the middle; causes and preventive actions above; the symptoms you'd observe below; a mirror-image model for success.

When the room challenges a finding

  1. AcknowledgeSay the alternative explanation out loud.
  2. Show the checkSay what you tested — e.g., recall counts, not just units.
  3. Restate the mechanismWhat you believe is happening, and why.
  4. Say what would change your mindOffer to dig: “I'll take these questions and run them through.”

How causal thinking differentiates our message

The usual messageThe LNS message
“91% have adopted or plan to adopt AI.”→Adoption is universal, so it can't explain who wins. Here's what the top 15% do with it.
“Leaders do more across more swim lanes.”→Discipline lets leaders scale each deployment; breadth follows.
“Best practice: push decisions down.”→Push decisions down — once expertise and decision rights are defined.
“Get OEE to world class.”→Past parity, OEE stops paying. Agility is where margin moves.
“Here's how you benchmark.”→Here's what to stop doing, and what breaks if you do.
Why CDI is built on causal models: causes plus conditions are what let a third-party insight travel into one company's context.
